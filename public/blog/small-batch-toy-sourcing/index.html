Small Batch Toy Sourcing: 100–500 pcs Without Getting Ignored

Factories quote 1,000 pcs minimum because small orders are unprofitable for them, not because they dislike you. Five ways to get 100–500 pcs at a workable price, plus the real premium to expect.

You send an inquiry for 200 plush toys. Three factories do not reply. The fourth replies with a 1,000-piece minimum and a price 60% above the quote you saw online. You conclude that Chinese factories do not want small business.

They do want it. They just cannot run a production line profitably at 200 pieces, and most do not have a polite way to say so. Understanding the arithmetic changes what you ask for.

Why small orders get ignored#

A toy factory’s cost per order is mostly fixed:

Fixed costApproximateNotes
Production line setupUSD 150–400Changing fabric, thread, mould
Pattern / artwork preparationUSD 100–300Even for a repeat design
Material minimum purchaseUSD 200–800Fabric and trims sold by the roll/box
QC and packing setupUSD 80–200Inspection plan, carton spec
Export documentationUSD 60–150Per shipment, regardless of size
Fixed totalUSD 600–1,800Before a single unit is made

At 1,000 pieces that is USD 0.60–1.80 per unit. At 200 pieces it is USD 3.00–9.00 per unit. The factory is not being greedy — a 200-piece order at the 1,000-piece price would be sold at a loss.

This is also why a 200-piece quote often looks absurd. The factory is not padding the price; it is spreading the same fixed cost over one-fifth of the volume.

What is realistic at each volume#

VolumeRealistic outcomeExpect to pay
50–99 pcsStock items only, or sample-level pricingFOB + 50–100%
100–199 pcsPossible with a cooperative factory on a simple designFOB + 30–60%
200–299 pcsWorkable for most categories with negotiationFOB + 20–40%
300–499 pcsNormal small-order range, most factories will quoteFOB + 10–25%
500+ pcsStandard territoryFOB + 0–10%

These premiums are not arbitrary. They map roughly to the fixed-cost table above. A factory quoting 200 pieces at only 10% above the 1,000-piece price is either making an error or planning to substitute materials.

Five ways to get to 100–500 pcs#

1. Buy stock, not custom (cheapest and fastest)

If your goal is to test whether a product sells, do not customise. Buy an existing stock SKU in small quantity, with your logo on a hang tag or a sticker on the polybag. Many suppliers hold stock at 1 set or 10 pcs. You get a real product in 7 days at near-wholesale pricing, and you learn your sell-through before committing to a mould.

This is the right answer for roughly half of the people who ask us for 200 custom pieces. See stock plush toys as an example of the model.

2. Consolidate with other items (spread the fixed cost)

The fixed cost is per order, not per SKU. If you order 200 pcs of three designs from the same factory, the setup cost is shared across 600 units. Ask explicitly:

Can we combine three SKUs into one production run to reach your minimum?

Many factories will accept 600 total units across three designs where they would refuse 200 of one.

3. Use an agent’s existing order flow (piggyback)

An agent running regular orders with a factory can add your 200 pieces to a production slot that is already being set up. Your marginal cost is materials plus a small handling fee, because the setup is already paid for. This is the single most effective route for genuinely small first orders, and it is a large part of what a sourcing agent actually does.

4. Simplify the design to hit the minimum

A plain round plush with embroidered eyes has a far lower setup cost than a shaped character with printed fabric, appliqué and a 3D nose. If 200 pieces is the constraint, choose the design the factory can run cheaply. Ask: “What is the lowest-MOQ version of this product you can make?” You will often get a different answer than the one in the catalogue.

5. Pay the setup cost separately

Some factories will accept 200 pieces if you pay tooling, plates and pattern-making as a separate one-off charge instead of amortising it. You pay more up front, but you own the pattern and the per-unit price stays honest. Clarify ownership in writing — see sample and tooling ownership.

The inquiry that actually gets a reply#

Most small-buyer inquiries fail because they ask a question the factory cannot answer cheaply. Compare:

What gets ignored:

Hi, I need 200 plush toys, please send your best price.

What gets a reply:

Subject: 200 pcs + 400 pcs repeat — plush bear, EN71

We are a new brand testing the market. Target: 200 pcs for a test order, with a 400–600 pcs repeat if sell-through is good.

Product: 25 cm plush bear, short-pile fabric, PP cotton, embroidered eyes. Packaging: polybag with printed header card. Testing: EN71-1/-2/-3 reports required.

Please tell us: (1) your MOQ for this exact spec, (2) whether a 200 pcs order is possible at a premium, and (3) the per-unit price at 200, 400 and 1,000 pcs.

Three things make this work: it states the repeat intent, it gives a complete spec so the factory does not have to guess, and it asks for a price ladder so you can see exactly what the small-order premium is. That last point is also a negotiation tool — if 200 pcs costs 40% more than 400 pcs, you may prefer to start at 400.

Three traps in small-batch sourcing#

Trap 1: accepting a low price with a vague spec. The factory that says “yes, 200 pieces, no problem” and quotes the same price as 1,000 pieces is planning to substitute fabric or filling. Ask for g/m² and filling weight per piece.

Trap 2: shipping 200 pieces by air and calling it sourcing. Air freight on a small order can double the landed cost. For a test order, price the freight before you approve the order. See landed cost.

Trap 3: ordering 200 pieces across five designs. Splitting a small order across many SKUs multiplies the fixed cost five times. Small batch plus wide assortment is the most expensive combination in toy importing.

After the test order#

Plan the second order before you place the first, because it determines how the factory treats you. Two commitments make a real difference:

  • Give a target repeat quantity and a date. “If this sells, we will reorder 800 pcs in March” converts you from a nuisance order to a pipeline.
  • Ask what changes at 500 and 1,000 pcs. It tells you the factory’s price ladder and where the step-downs are.

Small buyers who communicate a plan get treated as future volume. Small buyers who ask for one cheap order get treated as one cheap order.

When small batch is the wrong answer#

If your volume is genuinely under 100 pieces and the product must be custom, you are usually better off:

  • buying a stock item and rebranding the packaging,
  • running a pre-order campaign to prove demand before producing, or
  • choosing a category with lower minimums (fidget and pet toys often start at 1 set or 10 pcs, versus 500 for custom plush).

We quote small orders routinely where the category allows it — the MOQ table in llms.txt lists starting quantities per category. If your target is 200 pcs of a custom plush, tell us the spec and the repeat plan, and we will tell you honestly whether the economics work or whether a stock item is the smarter test.
