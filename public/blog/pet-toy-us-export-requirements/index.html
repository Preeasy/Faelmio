Pet Toy Export to the US: What Actually Applies

Pet toys are not covered by CPSIA, which surprises most buyers. Here is what does apply — FDA feed rules for edible chews, state chemical laws, Amazon documentation, and labeling.

  • What to ask your supplier for
  • Summary

Most buyers assume pet toys are regulated like children’s toys. They are not — and that misunderstanding cuts both ways. It makes some buyers spend money on certificates nobody asks for, and it lets others ship products that get stopped at the border or pulled from Amazon.

Here is the accurate picture for pet toys entering the United States.

The key distinction: pet toys are not children’s products#

The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act (CPSIA) applies to products designed or intended primarily for children 12 and under. A dog chew toy is not a children’s product. That means:

  • No Children’s Product Certificate (CPC) is required
  • No third-party CPSIA testing is mandated
  • CPSIA phthalate and lead limits do not legally apply

This is a genuine difference from a plush toy sold to a three-year-old. If a supplier tells you a pet toy “needs a CPC”, they are either misunderstanding the rule or reusing a children’s-toy template.

The exception that catches people: a product marketed to children and pets — a plush animal sold as a children’s toy that a dog also plays with — is a children’s product and CPSIA applies in full. Marketing copy determines classification. Do not describe a pet product as suitable for toddlers.

What does apply#

1. FDA rules for edible chews and treats

If the product is intended to be eaten — rawhide, dental chews with digestible content, treats, anything with a nutritional claim — it falls under FDA animal food regulation, not toy regulation. That brings facility registration, prior notice for imports, and labeling requirements. A rubber chew toy does not. A chew with a treat pocket where the treat is sold separately usually does not either, but the labeling must be clear that the toy is not food.

2. State chemical laws

California Proposition 65 is the one that actually triggers claims. It requires a warning for exposure to listed chemicals above threshold. For pet products the practical exposure is:

  • Phthalates in soft PVC or TPR
  • Lead in painted or coated parts
  • Diisocyanates in polyurethane foam

A Prop 65 warning label is not a defect — it is a disclosure. Many large retailers simply refuse products that require one, which is the real commercial constraint. Ask for a phthalate-free and lead-free formulation and get test data, rather than accepting a warning label and losing the account.

Several other states (Washington, Maine, Vermont, New York) have their own chemical reporting rules that increasingly cover pet products. If your channel is a large US retailer, they will ask.

3. Flammability, if the product contains fabric or foam

There is no federal pet-toy flammability standard. But if you sell a plush or foam pet bed or a stuffed pet toy through a retailer with its own standards policy, they may apply ASTM F963 or California TB 117 testing by contract, even though the law does not require it. Read the retailer’s vendor requirements, not just the law.

4. Amazon and other marketplace requirements

This is where most sellers actually get blocked. Amazon requires, for pet supplies:

  • Product safety documentation on request, typically a test report from an ISO 17025-accredited lab
  • Country of origin labeling
  • Allergen and material disclosure in some categories

Amazon does not publish a fixed list of required tests for pet toys, and requirements change. Practical approach: get a test report covering heavy metals, phthalates and azo dyes from an accredited lab before you list. It costs a few hundred dollars per SKU and clears most requests.

Labeling that is actually required#

Federal law does not mandate a specific pet-toy label. What you should still put on retail packaging:

  • Country of origin (“Made in China”) — required for imported goods under US Customs rules
  • Manufacturer or importer name and address — required for most consumer goods
  • Material content, especially for chew-resistant claims
  • “Not a children’s toy” if there is any chance of the product being used by a child
  • Warnings: “Remove toy if damaged”, “Not for aggressive chewers”, “Supervise use”

The “not for aggressive chewers” line is commercially important. Most pet-toy returns are from a dog destroying a product in ten minutes, and a clear chew-strength claim reduces damage claims.

Chew strength is the real quality spec#

For pet toys, the specification that matters more than any certificate is chew strength, and it is the one Chinese factories most often leave vague. Define it:

LevelTypical useMaterial
Soft / plushGentle chewers, puppies, fetchPlush with minimal filling, no squeaker
MediumAverage adult dogsTPR, natural rubber, layered fabric
ToughStrong chewersDense natural rubber, nylon core
Power chewerAggressive destroyersSolid nylon, hard rubber, no fabric

Test it by sending samples to real dogs, or ask the factory for a tensile and tear test report. Our pet toy range is specified by chew level rather than by “durable” as a claim.

What to ask your supplier for#

1. Material composition, by part, with % content
2. Phthalate and heavy-metal test report, accredited lab, current batch
3. Country of origin marking on product and carton
4. Chew strength rating and the test method used
5. Whether any part is edible, and if so, FDA compliance documentation
6. Retail packaging artwork approval before print

That list is short, and on a first order it separates suppliers who understand the US market from those who are reselling a domestic product spec.

Summary#

  • Pet toys are not CPSIA children’s products; no CPC is required
  • Edible chews move into FDA animal food territory
  • Prop 65 and state chemical laws are the real claim risk
  • Amazon will ask for accredited-lab reports; get them before listing
  • Chew strength is the specification that decides return rates
