Japan ST Mark and Korea KC: Selling Toys into Japan and Korea

Japan and Korea both test toys for the same hazards as the EU and US — but neither accepts an EN71 or CPC report. What the ST Mark and KC confirmation actually require, who can apply, and how to plan the labels.

Japan and Korea are the two Asian markets most buyers underestimate. Both have mature toy regulations, both test for the same hazards as the EU and US, and neither will accept an EN71 report or a CPC as evidence.

The practical difference is who carries the paperwork. In the EU you appoint a responsible person; in Japan and Korea the importer is the accountable party, and in Korea the certificate must be held by a Korean-registered business. That single fact determines how you structure the shipment.

Japan: the ST Mark#

What it is#

The ST Mark (STマーク) is the toy safety mark administered by the Japan Toy Association (JTA). It is voluntary under law but effectively mandatory in commerce: major retailers, e-commerce platforms and distributors expect it, and a toy without it is difficult to place in any serious channel.

The ST Standard is published in three parts:

PartCovers
Part 1Physical and mechanical properties — sharp edges, small parts, cords, entrapment
Part 2Flammability — burn rate for soft and plush toys, surface flash
Part 3Chemical properties — heavy metals, phthalates, formaldehyde, dye migration

Age divisions follow the same logic as EN 71: under 36 months, 3–6, 6–14 and 14+. The age symbol printed with the mark tells the consumer which group the toy was assessed for, and it has to match the toy’s actual design and warnings.

The legal layer underneath#

The ST Mark is not the only requirement. Three laws can apply on top of it:

  • Consumer Product Safety Act (CPSA). Certain categories require the PSC mark and a business notification to METI. Some infant and toddler toys and magnet-based play products fall into this group.
  • Food Sanitation Act. Toys that infants may put in their mouths — teethers, rattles, some soft toys — are regulated for material safety, and imports are notified at the quarantine station.
  • PSE and the Radio Act. Battery toys are outside PSE, but any mains adapter needs PSE. Bluetooth or radio-controlled toys need the technical conformity mark (技適).

A toy can therefore need the ST Mark plus one or more of these, and each has its own testing and labelling.

Who applies, and how long it takes#

The ST Mark is licensed through the Japan Toy Association, and testing is done by designated institutes. Your Japanese importer or distributor is normally the licence holder; a Chinese factory cannot hold it on your behalf.

Budget roughly 3–5 weeks for testing plus admin for one model family, with costs in the low thousands of US dollars per family depending on the number of parts and colours tested. A “model family” — same construction, same materials, different colours or sizes — reduces cost substantially compared with testing each SKU separately, so group your range deliberately before you send samples.

Label requirements in practice#

Japanese retailers will reject a carton for label issues faster than for almost anything else. The sewn-in or applied label should carry:

  • ST Mark with the correct age symbol
  • Manufacturer or importer name and address (in Japanese)
  • Country of origin
  • Target age and the required warnings in Japanese
  • Batch or lot identification

The Japanese warning text is conventional and short — “対象年齢” plus the specific hazard statement — but it must be present and legible at retail size. Approve the label artwork with your importer before printing.

Korea: KC confirmation for children’s products#

What it is#

Korea regulates toys under the Special Act on Safety Management of Children’s Products. Toys are “children’s products”, and most toys sit in the safety confirmation (안전확인) route: a sample is tested by an accredited laboratory, a report is issued, and the product is registered with the KC mark and a confirmation number.

The KC mark is not optional. Without it the product cannot be customs-cleared for sale or listed on Korean platforms.

What gets tested#

Korean toy testing overlaps heavily with EN 71 but is not identical:

  • Physical and mechanical hazards, including small parts for under-36-month toys
  • Flammability
  • Heavy metal migration and phthalates
  • Labelling and warnings in Korean

Because the standards are national, an existing EN71 or ASTM report shortens the work — your lab can reuse data — but it does not replace the Korean report.

Who can apply#

This is the constraint that catches first-time sellers. The certificate holder must be a Korean-registered business. A Chinese manufacturer or a foreign brand cannot hold it directly. In practice you have three options:

  1. Sell to a Korean importer who holds the confirmation and re-labels under their own name.
  2. Appoint a Korean distributor as the applicant, with a written agreement about who owns the mark and the test data.
  3. Use a Korean compliance agency that acts as the applicant and importer of record, which is workable but adds a party to the supply chain.

Decide this before you quote, because it changes the Incoterms and the price. Selling DDP to a Korean warehouse is not the same as selling to an importer who will hold the KC.

Label requirements#

Korean labels are strict and specific. Expect to show:

  • KC mark and the safety confirmation number
  • Product name and model
  • Manufacturer name and address, plus the importer’s name and address
  • Country of origin
  • Manufacturing date (제조년월) — a month and year, not just a year
  • Age range and warnings in Korean
  • Weight or quantity where applicable

The manufacturing date is the field most often missing from Chinese factory labels, because it is not a common requirement elsewhere. Add it to the label artwork from the first draft.

The pattern is consistent: each market wants its own report, its own mark and a label in its own language, held by a party established in that market.

Planning the sequence#

For a toy going to both countries, the order that avoids rework:

  1. Lock the design and materials first. Every change after testing means a new report.
  2. Send samples to both labs at the same time. The tests overlap; the reports do not.
  3. Confirm the applicant in each market — importer in Japan, Korean business in Korea — before testing starts, because the certificate is issued to them.
  4. Draft both labels in parallel. The Korean label needs the manufacturing month; the Japanese label needs the age symbol. Both need the importer’s address, which you only know once step 3 is settled.
  5. Test, then print. Never print labels on the assumption that testing will pass. Plastic parts and printed inks are the usual failures, and they are fixable — after the report, not before.
  6. Ship. Keep the report numbers, the confirmation number and the label artwork in the technical file alongside your EN71 or CPC documents.

The mistakes that cost the most#

  • Assuming an EN71 report will be accepted. It will not, in either market. It may reduce the test scope, nothing more.
  • Testing one colour of a multi-colour range. Korean and Japanese labs commonly require each colourway, because pigments and plasticisers differ. Group SKUs by material, not by artwork.
  • Forgetting the importer on the label. A label with only the Chinese factory address fails in both countries.
  • Printing labels before the test report. A failed phthalate test on a printed part means reprinting every carton.
  • Treating ST as optional because it is voluntary. Retailers enforce it; a toy without the mark simply does not get shelf space.

The short version#

  • Japan: ST Mark from the Japan Toy Association — voluntary in law, required in practice, licensed through your Japanese importer.
  • Korea: KC safety confirmation under the children’s products act — mandatory, and the certificate must be held by a Korean-registered business.
  • Neither market accepts an EN71 report or a CPC; both need local testing and local-language labels.
  • Korea requires the manufacturing month on the label; Japan requires the ST age symbol.
  • Settle the applicant and the label artwork before testing, and group your SKUs by material to control cost.
