Payment Terms with Chinese Toy Factories: T/T, L/C, and When to Walk Away

30/70 T/T is standard, but the details decide who carries the risk. How payment terms work with Chinese toy factories, the red flags of bank-fraud scams, and when to refuse.

Almost every toy order from China runs on a deposit. The interesting question is not whether you pay 30% up front — it is what happens to the other 70%, who verifies the goods, and what the factory can do if you refuse to pay.

Payment terms are where trust is either built slowly or lost in a single transaction. Here is how the standard structures actually work, and the specific patterns that mean you should stop.

The five structures you will be offered#

StructureTypical useRisk sits with
30% deposit / 70% before shipmentStandard for most toy ordersBuyer (until goods ship)
30% deposit / 70% after inspectionPreferred by experienced buyersShared
100% T/T in advanceSmall orders, first-time, low valueBuyer entirely
L/C at sightOrders above USD 50,000–100,000Bank-mediated
Trade Assurance / escrowAlibaba and similar platformsPlatform (partially)

30/70 before shipment is the default because it protects the factory’s cash flow: materials must be bought before production. The buyer’s protection comes from inspection before the balance is paid — which only works if you actually inspect, or appoint someone who does.

30/70 after inspection is the structure to ask for on any order above roughly USD 3,000. It reads: deposit on PO, balance released after the pre-shipment inspection report is accepted. Most factories accept this; a factory that refuses it on a first order is telling you something.

100% in advance is normal for stock items under a few hundred dollars and for sample orders. Above that, it is a red flag unless you have a long relationship.

L/C at sight costs 0.5–1.5% in bank fees and requires precise documentation — one typo can delay payment by weeks. It is worth it for large orders with a new factory, and unnecessary for small ones.

Trade Assurance gives you a platform-mediated dispute process. It is genuinely useful for a first order, but the coverage is limited to the terms recorded in the order, so the specification must be written into the order itself, not agreed by chat.

What the deposit actually covers#

The 30% is not a security deposit. It is working capital for:

  • Fabric and trims purchased up front
  • Mould or plate costs, if any
  • Labour scheduled on a production line
  • Packaging printed before assembly

If you cancel after the deposit is paid, the factory has already spent part of it. This is why deposits are usually non-refundable, and why the contract should say what happens to unused materials if an order is cancelled mid-production.

The clause that matters more than the percentage#

The payment split protects nobody if the specification is vague. Put the acceptance criteria in the PO:

1. Deposit: 30% on PO signature, T/T.
2. Balance: 70% within 5 working days after buyer accepts the pre-shipment
   inspection report (AQL II, major 2.5 / minor 4.0).
3. Inspection: third-party or buyer-appointed, at factory, 7 days before
   scheduled shipment.
4. If inspection fails: factory reworks and re-inspects at its own cost.
   Buyer may cancel if a second inspection fails, with deposit refunded
   less material actually consumed.
5. Late delivery: 0.5% of order value per week, capped at 5%.
6. Golden sample signed by both parties is the quality reference.

Item 2 is the one that changes behaviour. When the balance depends on an inspection result, the factory manages quality upstream instead of hoping.

Bank fraud: the risk nobody warns you about#

Business email compromise is common in Chinese sourcing, and it targets exactly this moment: the week before you send the 70%.

The pattern:

  1. You agree terms with a real factory, using a real email address.
  2. Weeks later, an email arrives from a near-identical address — one character different, or the display name matches but the domain is factory-co.com instead of factory.com — saying the company has changed banks and the balance should go to a new account.
  3. The account is real, in Hong Kong or Shenzhen, and the money is gone.

The defence is mechanical, not clever:

  • Verify any bank change by phone, calling a number you already had — never a number in the email.
  • Compare the account name to the company name on the contract. A mismatch (paying a personal name or a third-party trading company) requires an explanation in writing.
  • Never accept a bank change sent only by email, even from a correct address. Treat it as fraud until confirmed by voice.
  • Pay the deposit and balance to the same account unless there is a documented reason.

We have seen buyers lose a full 70% balance this way. It is the single most expensive mistake in the whole sourcing process, and it is entirely preventable with one phone call.

Red flags that mean stop#

SignalWhy it matters
Insists on 100% advance on a first order above USD 3,000Transfers all risk to you
Requests payment to a personal accountNo legal recourse, likely tax/export irregularity
Changes bank details by email onlyClassic BEC fraud pattern
Refuses third-party inspectionHas something to hide, or cannot meet spec
No business licence or export rightsCannot legally export; goods may be seized
Price far below market with no explanationMaterial substitution is coming
Asks for payment in cryptoNo traceability, no dispute process

The last two are also a signal about how the rest of the relationship will go. A factory that cuts corners on payment paperwork cuts corners on fabric weight.

When to walk away#

Walk away — not negotiate — when:

  1. A bank change cannot be confirmed by phone with a number you already had. No exceptions.
  2. The company name on the invoice does not match the payee, and the explanation is verbal only.
  3. They refuse inspection but want the balance before loading. You are being asked to pay blind.
  4. They demand 100% advance on a first order over USD 3,000 and will not accept 30/70 with inspection.

In each case, there are hundreds of other factories. The cost of finding another supplier is a week. The cost of a bad payment is the entire order.

A workable default#

For a first order with a new factory:

30% deposit on PO
70% after third-party AQL inspection, before shipment
Third-party inspection cost: USD 250–350/day (Zhejiang)
Payment to the company named on the contract, verified by phone
Golden sample signed and retained by both parties
Late-delivery penalty in the PO

This structure is standard enough that reputable factories accept it without argument, and strict enough that the ones who refuse are telling you what you need to know. Our how to order page documents the version we use on every order, including what happens if inspection fails twice.
