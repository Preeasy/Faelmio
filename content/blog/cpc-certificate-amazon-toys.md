If you sell toys on Amazon US, the CPC is the document that decides whether your listing survives. It is also the document most often submitted wrong.

This is not legal advice. Requirements change — confirm the current rules with a CPSC-accepted lab before you ship.

## What a CPC actually is

A Children's Product Certificate is a self-issued document. The **importer or manufacturer** issues it — not the testing lab. The lab produces the test report; you turn that report into a certificate.

That distinction is where most sellers fail. They send Amazon the lab's report and assume it is the CPC. It is not, and Amazon's compliance team will reject it.

A valid CPC names:

- The product and the specific model/SKU it covers
- The US importer or domestic manufacturer responsible
- The date and place of manufacture
- The date and place of testing
- The CPSC-accepted third-party lab that performed the test
- Each rule the product was tested against, with the citation
- Contact details for the records custodian

If any of those is missing, it is not a CPC.

## Which rules a toy is tested against

For a toy sold in the US, a CPC normally cites:

| Rule | What it covers |
|---|---|
| 16 CFR 1250 (ASTM F963-23) | Toy safety: mechanical, physical, flammability, heavy metals |
| 16 CFR 1303 | Lead in surface coatings (90 ppm) |
| 16 CFR 1307 | Phthalates (8 restricted, 0.1% each) |
| 16 CFR 1501 | Small parts — choking hazard for under 3 |
| 16 CFR 1500.50–.53 | Use and abuse testing (drop, torque, tension) |

ASTM F963-23 is the current version. A certificate citing F963-16 or F963-11 is out of date and can be rejected on that basis alone.

## The five rejection reasons we see most

**1. The certificate is not tied to the ASIN.** Amazon wants the model number on the CPC to match the listing. A generic "plush toy" certificate covering a whole factory's output does not clear a specific listing.

**2. The lab is not CPSC-accepted.** Only labs accepted under CPSC's programme can do children's product testing. A certificate from a domestic Chinese lab without that acceptance is worthless for US import.

**3. The importer field is blank or wrong.** If you are the importer of record, your company name must be on it — not the factory's, not your agent's.

**4. Age grading does not match the listing.** A product tested as 3+ but listed as 0+ is a mismatch, and Amazon treats it as a compliance failure rather than a typo.

**5. The test date predates a material change.** Changed the paint supplier, the fabric, or the mould? The old report no longer covers the product.

## What it costs and how long it takes

For a straightforward toy, third-party testing to F963 plus CPSIA rules typically runs **USD 300–800 per SKU** and **5–10 working days**, depending on how many colours and materials are involved. Complex products with electronics, batteries or multiple fabrics cost more.

A factory that already sells into the US usually has reports for its stock items. If they cannot produce one within a day, they probably do not export to the US regularly — which is itself useful information.

## The trap: certificates that exist but do not cover your order

A factory shows you a CE certificate and a CPC. Both look official. Then you read them:

- The CE certificate is an EN71 test report from 2019, for a product with the same name but a different size
- The CPC names a different importer — the factory's previous US customer
- The model number is the factory's internal code, not your SKU

None of these will clear Amazon. Always ask for the **test report**, not just the certificate. The report has the sample photos, the test dates and the actual measured values; the certificate is a one-page summary that anyone can retype.

## What to ask your factory for

Before you place a deposit, request:

1. The full test report (PDF, not a scan of a certificate)
2. The lab's CPSC acceptance number
3. Confirmation of which SKU and which materials were tested
4. Whether any component comes from a different supplier — paint, eyes, filling, batteries

Point 4 matters more than buyers expect. If the eyes are bought in from another factory and that factory changes its supplier, your report may no longer be valid even though nothing about your order changed.

## Where this fits in a sourcing relationship

A sourcing agent who ships toys to the US should be able to tell you, before you order, which lab the factory uses and whether the report covers your SKU. If they cannot, they are moving boxes, not managing compliance.
