Roughly half of the suppliers presenting themselves as factories on marketplaces and trade shows are trading companies. That is not automatically a problem — some trading companies are excellent, and many factories sell through them deliberately. It becomes a problem when you are paying factory prices for an intermediary, or when a quality issue needs someone who can walk onto the production floor.

These nine checks take about a week and cost almost nothing.

## The documents

**1. Business licence (营业执照)**
Ask for the unified social credit code (统一社会信用代码). It is a public identifier and can be checked on China's National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System. What you are looking for: does the registered scope include manufacturing of toys, or only sales?

**2. Export licence**
A company without an export licence cannot ship in its own name and will route your order through a third party. That is a layer you should know about.

**3. Product liability insurance**
Rarely asked for and very informative. A factory that carries it has exposure to Western retailers and has thought about recalls.

**4. Test reports in the factory's own name**
Not "our client tested it". If a factory has never held a report in its own name, it has never been the responsible supplier to a regulated market.

## The physical evidence

**5. A video call with the machines running**
This is the single most useful check. Ask for a walk-through at a time you choose, with the line running. Specific requests that are hard to fake:

- Pan from the entrance sign to the production floor without cutting
- Show the moulding machines with the counter reading
- Show finished cartons with a visible shipping mark
- Show the QC area and the inspection records on the desk

A trading company will offer a pre-recorded video, or a call from an office. A factory will just walk outside.

**6. Ask where the product is actually made**
If the answer is "our partner factory", you are talking to a trading company. Then ask which factory, and whether you can visit it. The answer tells you whether the relationship is transparent or extractive.

**7. Capacity arithmetic**
Ask: how many sets per day, on how many lines, at how many shifts? Then compare with your order and lead time. A factory quoting 500,000 units in 20 days with two machines is either lying or planning to subcontract.

## The commercial signals

**8. How they price a small order**
A real factory will quote a higher unit price for 300 pieces and explain why. A trading company will often quote the same low price and then discover problems later. A suspiciously flat price across volumes is a warning.

**9. Who answers the technical questions**
Send a technical question — material grade, mould material, drop-test height, carton compression. If every question routes back to "the engineer will confirm", that is normal for a large factory. If the answer arrives as a copy-paste with no adaptation to your product, note it.

## The two questions that reveal the most

**"Which of your customers in our market can we speak to?"**
Many factories will decline, and that is legitimate — customer confidentiality is real. But how they decline is informative. "We cannot share names, but here is the market and the volume we ship there" is a professional answer.

**"What went wrong on your last shipment, and what did you change?"**
A factory that answers "nothing" is either new, lying, or not paying attention. A good answer names a specific problem — a colour batch, a carton failure, a late raw material — and a process change.

## What an audit does not tell you

A passed audit is a snapshot. Factories change staff, lose key customers, and take on too much work in peak season. The most common failure mode is not a bad factory — it is a good factory that is overbooked in September and lets your order slip.

This is why the inspection before shipment matters more than the audit before the order. See [quality control](/quality-compliance/) for what we check at each stage.

## Practical sequencing

1. Documents first — a week, costs nothing, eliminates the obvious mismatches
2. Video call — 30 minutes, eliminates half of what remains
3. Sample order — the only test that matters
4. Third-party inspection on the first bulk shipment — non-negotiable for retail and Amazon
5. Audit in person once you are ordering regularly

Skipping straight to step 3 is why buyers lose deposits. The documents in step 1 are free and public.
