Plush is the category where quotes diverge the most — three suppliers, three prices, sometimes a 3x spread. The reason is rarely the factory's margin. It is that "a 25 cm teddy bear in soft fabric" describes a product with perhaps forty undefined variables, and every factory fills the gaps differently.

This guide covers the three that move cost and safety most: fabric, filling and construction. It ends with the inspection checks that tell you whether you received what you specified.

## Fabric: the four numbers that define it

Ask for these explicitly. A supplier who cannot answer them is quoting from a catalogue, not from a specification.

| Parameter | Typical range | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Fibre content | 100% polyester (most common); acrylic; cotton blends | Determines feel, pilling, dye behaviour and price |
| Weight | 180–450 g/m² | The single biggest cost driver after size; 220 vs 380 g/m² is a different product |
| Pile height | 1–20 mm (velboa 2–3 mm, long-pile 10–20 mm) | Visual "plushness"; long pile sheds and traps dust |
| Backing | Knitted or woven backing, with or without backing glue | Thin backing tears at seams; glue can fail at low temperature |

Two fabrics can look identical in a photo and differ by 40% in cost. Velboa at 250 g/m² is the workhorse for toys under about 25 cm. Long-pile "fur" at 500 g/m² and above is what makes a large bear feel expensive — and it is also the fabric that fails fire testing most often.

Ask for a **fabric swatch with the weight written on the label**, and keep it. When the bulk arrives, a GSM cutter (a circular punch and a scale) settles any dispute in two minutes.

### Dyed-lot and colour matching

Fabric is dyed in batches. If your order runs across two dye lots, the light pink of carton one and carton two may not match — and on a retail shelf that reads as a quality problem. For orders above roughly 2,000 pcs in one colour, specify "single dye lot" in the PO. Expect a surcharge if the mill has to hold the lot.

For a licensed or Pantone-matched colour, provide the Pantone reference and require a **lab-dip approval** before bulk fabric is cut. Approving colour from a phone photo is how returns happen.

## Filling: what is actually inside

Almost all plush filling is polyester fibre — "PP cotton" in factory language. The variables are grade and weight.

| Filling | Character | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Hollow conjugated siliconised fibre | Springs back, stays lofty after washing | Standard for quality plush |
| Solid fibre | Cheaper, packs down, lumps after washing | Low-cost promotional plush |
| Recycled PET fibre (rPET) | Same performance if specified correctly; needs certification | EU and US buyers with sustainability claims |
| Micro-bead / EPS pellets | Weighted feel (bean bags, "weighted" plush) | Niche; adds small-parts and labelling obligations |

Specify **fill weight in grams per unit**, not "stuffed firmly". A 30 cm bear typically takes 120–180 g; the same bear at 80 g feels hollow within a week. Ask the factory to weigh the first article on a scale and record it — that number goes into the inspection checklist.

Two failure modes are common and both are invisible until the customer opens the box:

- **Offcut filling.** Some low-cost factories stuff with fabric offcuts or non-woven waste. It smells, it clumps, and it can contain loose fibres that fail small-parts assessment. Require "100% polyester fibre filling, no offcuts" in writing.
- **Under-filling to hit a price.** A quote that is 15% below the others is often 30% less filling. Since filling is cheap relative to fabric, this is a poor trade — but it is the easiest one for a factory to make silently.

If you make an rPET or recycled-content claim, the fibre supplier's certification (GRS or equivalent) must be in the technical file. A claim without a certificate is a compliance risk, not a marketing asset.

## Construction: where safety lives

### Eyes, noses and attached parts

This is the detail that decides whether a toy can be sold to children under three.

| Option | Safety profile | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Embroidered eyes and nose | No detachable part; safest for 0–3 | Higher labour, lower material |
| Safety eyes with washer lock | Passes tension testing when correctly set | Low |
| Glued or screw-on eyes | Should not be used on children's plush | Low, and not worth the risk |
| Plastic nose with prongs | Acceptable if it passes pull testing | Low |

EN 71-1 applies a **90 N tension and 0.34 N·m torque** test to components that a child could grip; ASTM F963 uses comparable pull and torque tests. Whether a safety eye passes depends less on the eye than on the washer being properly seated and the fabric not tearing around it. That is a factory skill, and it is why plush from an unverified workshop is a gamble even when the components are correct.

For anything labelled 0–3, embroidered features remove the question entirely. We default to embroidery on infant plush for that reason.

### Seams and stitching

- **Seam type:** overlock or flatlock for the body, back-stitch at stress points (arm and leg joins, neck).
- **Stitch density:** 8–12 stitches per inch. Too loose and the filling escapes; too tight and the fabric puckers.
- **Seam strength:** the seam should not open under a firm manual pull at the arm and leg joins. Ask for the first article to be pull-tested and photographed.
- **Closing seam:** the final closure (usually the bottom) is the weakest point. It must be hand-stitched with a locked finish, not spot-glued.

### Embellishments

Appliqué, heat-transfer print and embroidery all add cost and all add failure points. Heat-transfer prints crack after washing; specify wash testing at 40 °C if the toy is machine washable. Sequins, ribbons and bows on infant toys are a small-parts risk and should be avoided below three years.

## Fire safety: EN 71-2 and ASTM F963

Plush is a soft toy, and soft toys are tested for flammability in both major markets.

**EN 71-2** assesses the rate at which flame spreads across the surface. Soft toys must not exceed the standard's maximum burn rate — in the current version, 30 mm/s — and surface flash is also assessed. Long-pile and brushed fabrics are the ones that fail; dense velboa at 250–300 g/m² generally passes without treatment.

**ASTM F963 Section 4.2** sets a burn-rate limit for soft toys in the US market. In practice most polyester plush passes, but acrylic pile and very open, fluffy constructions are the risk cases.

Two practical points:

1. **Test the actual fabric, not a similar one.** A supplier who says "our fabric already passed EN71-2" is telling you about a different roll. The test report must reference the fabric weight and composition you are buying.
2. **Do not accept a flame retardant as the fix without asking what it is.** Some FR chemistries are restricted under REACH or create their own compliance problem. Changing the fabric is usually cleaner than treating it.

Always confirm current test parameters with your lab before you commit to a report — standards are amended, and the version matters for a CPC or DoC.

## Chemical and labelling obligations

Plush is mouthable by design, which brings the chemical tests into scope:

- **EN 71-3** — migration of heavy metals from the fabric, prints and plastic parts.
- **REACH Annex XVII** — azo dyes, formaldehyde, and restricted substances in the textile.
- **CPSIA** — total lead limit (100 ppm in substrate), phthalates at 0.1% in plasticised parts, plus a CPC for US import.
- **Age grading and warnings** — the CE mark, the age symbol (0–3 or 3+), and the "not suitable for children under 36 months" warning where applicable, in the language of the sale country.

Label artwork is printed on the sewn-in label and often on the hangtag, so mistakes are expensive to correct. Approve the artwork before the labels are produced, not after.

## The five inspection checks that catch bad plush

At AQL Level II pre-shipment, add these five to the standard checklist:

1. **Fabric weight.** Cut a 100 cm² sample from a random unit, weigh it, compare to the approved swatch. Below 90% of spec is a fail.
2. **Fill weight.** Weigh three units per size and compare to the first-article record.
3. **Pull test.** Apply a firm manual pull to eyes, noses and the closing seam on a sample of units.
4. **Needle and metal detection.** Every plush line should pass through a metal detector, with a documented broken-needle policy. This is standard for plush and is skipped by small workshops.
5. **Wash test.** Wash one unit at the labelled temperature and check for colour bleeding, pilling, filling clumping and print cracking.

The rest is standard: label text, barcode scan, carton drop, packing count, and visual defects against the approved sample.

## How to write the spec so quotes are comparable

A plush spec sheet that factories can quote from without guessing:

```text
Item: Teddy bear, sitting, 28 cm
Fabric: 100% polyester velboa, 280 g/m², colour Pantone 7529 C, head pile 3 mm
Filling: 100% polyester hollow siliconised fibre, 150 g per unit, no offcuts
Features: embroidered eyes and nose; embroidery on chest 4,000 stitches
Seams: overlock body, back-stitch at limb joins, hand-stitched locked closure
Age grade: 0+ (EN 71-1/2/3 and CPSIA testing required)
Label: sewn-in care label + hangtag, artwork approved before production
Packaging: 1 pc per polybag with suffocation warning, 40 pcs per export carton
Testing: EN 71-1/2/3 report for this fabric weight and colour
Target price: FOB Ningbo, 1,000 / 3,000 / 5,000 pcs
```

Send the same sheet to every supplier. The quotes you get back will then differ for real reasons — factory size, labour, fabric buying power — rather than for undefined specification. ## The short version

- Fabric weight in g/m² and pile height decide both cost and fire performance; put them in the PO.
- Specify filling grade and grams per unit — under-filling is the most common silent cost cut.
- Embroidered features are the safest choice for under-three plush; safety eyes depend on how the washer is set.
- EN 71-2 and ASTM F963 test the actual fabric, not a generic "plush" claim.
- Add fabric weight, fill weight, pull test, needle detection and a wash test to every plush inspection.
