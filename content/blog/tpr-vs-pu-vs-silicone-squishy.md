Three squishy balls can sit on the same table, look nearly identical, and differ by 400% in cost, 300% in lifespan and completely in how they feel after two weeks of use. The material is the whole product, and it is the one specification buyers most often leave blank.

Here is what actually separates the three materials you will be offered.

## Quick comparison

| | TPR | PU foam | Silicone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit cost (typical ball) | Low–mid | Lowest | Highest |
| Rebound speed | Slow, controlled | Fast | Slow, elastic |
| Feel | Rubbery, slightly tacky | Soft, spongy, dry | Silky, dense |
| Tear resistance | Good | Poor | Excellent |
| Odour out of the bag | Mild, fades in 2–3 days | Noticeable, fades in 1–2 weeks | Minimal |
| Surface finish | Can be coated/matte | Porous | Smooth, non-porous |
| Cleanability | Wipe only | Hard to clean | Washable |
| Best for | Squishy balls, fidgets | Cheap novelty, bulk promo | Premium, baby-adjacent, repeated use |
| Typical MOQ | 1,000–3,000 | 3,000+ | 500–1,000 |

## TPR (thermoplastic rubber)

TPR is the workhorse of the squishy category. It is injection-mouldable, cheap to tool, and can be tuned from very soft to firm by changing the formulation.

**Strengths.** Controlled slow rebound, which is what buyers mean by "squishy" — it returns to shape over two to five seconds rather than instantly. Easy to colour match. Can be coated with a wax layer to produce the "crunchy" or crispy fidget effect, as on our [crunchy 3-colour squishy ball](/toys/fidget-sensory-toys/FM-CST-Z1001A/).

**Weaknesses.** TPR is tacky by nature. Cheap formulations attract dust and can leave a slight residue on hands. It is also the material most likely to produce an initial plastic smell, because of the plasticiser. Reputable factories use low-VOC plasticisers and a post-production airing period; the smell should be gone within a few days of opening the carton.

**Tear behaviour.** TPR stretches before it tears. A fingernail or a pet's teeth will eventually puncture it, and once there is a hole the filling comes out, which is why the age grading matters more than the material.

## PU foam (polyurethane)

PU is the cheapest legitimate squishy material and the one behind most promotional stress balls.

**Strengths.** Very low unit cost, fast throughput, and it holds simple shapes well. Excellent for high-volume promotional items where the toy is a giveaway rather than a retail product.

**Weaknesses.** PU is porous and it degrades. The classic failure is surface cracking along the flex lines after a few thousand squeezes — the material fatigues rather than tears. It also tends to shed fine dust as it ages, which is a real issue for anything sold into a children's channel. Cleaning is impractical: washing accelerates degradation.

**Where PU is the right answer.** Orders above 5,000 pieces, promotional budgets under USD 0.50 landed, or products with a short intended life. Not for anything positioned as a durable toy.

## Silicone

Silicone is the premium option and increasingly the default for brands that get customer complaints about smell or tearing.

**Strengths.** Non-porous, so it does not absorb oils or dirt and can be washed. No plasticiser, so no plastic smell. High tear strength and it survives being stretched repeatedly. Best-in-class for repeated-use fidgets, teething-adjacent products and anything sold with a "premium" claim.

**Weaknesses.** Material cost is two to four times TPR. Moulding is slower and tooling is more expensive. Very soft silicone in large shapes can be difficult to demould cleanly, which pushes defect rates up on complex designs.

## The compliance angle

All three materials can be made to pass EN 71-1/-2/-3 and ASTM F963-23 — material choice does not exempt you from testing, but it changes what you need to test for.

- **Phthalates.** TPR and PVC-adjacent formulations are where phthalate risk concentrates. Ask for a phthalate test report (6P or 16P) specific to the batch.
- **Migration of elements.** Applies to all three, but coatings and pigments are the usual failure point, not the base polymer.
- **Small parts.** A torn squishy releases filling. If the product is age-graded 3+, you need a filled-and-sealed construction that resists a 70 N tensile pull on any attached part.

## How to specify material in a PO

Vague POs are how you end up with PU when you paid for TPR. Write it explicitly:

```
Material: TPR, Shore A 25 ±5, phthalate-free (6P), low-VOC plasticiser
Surface: matte, no tack residue after 24h at 23°C
Filling: soft clay / silicone gel (state which)
Test: EN71-1/-2/-3 + ASTM F963-23, reports from accredited lab
Odour: no perceptible odour after 72h airing
```

Add a golden sample signed by both parties and a clause that production must match it. This single paragraph prevents most material substitution disputes.

## A decision shortcut

- **Testing a new design on a tight budget** → TPR, 1,000–3,000 pcs.
- **Promotional giveaway, 5,000+ pcs** → PU, and accept a short life.
- **Retail product with repeat purchase, or claims about durability** → silicone.
- **Any product where a customer might complain about smell** → skip PU entirely.

If you are unsure, order the same design in two materials at sample stage. The USD 30–60 in samples is the cheapest market research available, and the difference in hand feel will settle the question faster than any specification sheet.
