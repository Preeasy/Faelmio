Buyers routinely ask for "the CE certificate" when they need four different documents, and for the wrong market. Here is what actually applies where.

Not legal advice. Rules change, and your lab or notified body is the authority for your specific product.

## The map

| Market | Standard | Who issues the document |
|---|---|---|
| European Union | EN71 (parts 1–3) under Toy Safety Directive 2009/48/EC | You self-declare, backed by a test report and technical file |
| European Union (2024 onwards) | GPSR — Regulation (EU) 2023/988 | You, plus a named EU responsible person |
| United States | ASTM F963-23 + CPSIA rules | CPC issued by the importer, based on third-party test report |
| United Kingdom | EN71 accepted alongside UKCA | You self-declare |
| Japan | ST Mark (voluntary, widely required by retail) | Third-party inspection body |
| Korea | KC Mark | Safety certification body |
| Australia / NZ | AS/NZS ISO 8124 | Self-declaration with test report |

## EN71: three parts, not one

EN71 is the European toy safety standard, and it is modular:

- **EN71-1** — mechanical and physical properties: sharp edges, small parts, cords, drop and tension tests
- **EN71-2** — flammability: how fast materials burn
- **EN71-3** — migration of elements: 19 heavy metals that can leach from materials a child mouths

A report that only covers EN71-1 is not a complete EN71 report. When a supplier sends "EN71 certified" and the document is eight pages covering only physical tests, ask for parts 2 and 3.

## CE marking is a declaration, not a certificate

There is no such thing as an official "CE certificate" for toys in the sense most buyers imagine. CE is the manufacturer's self-declaration that the product meets the directive. What backs it up is a technical file containing:

- The test reports
- Risk assessment
- Design and manufacturing drawings
- Material declarations from sub-suppliers
- Instructions and warnings
- The declaration of conformity itself

The declaration of conformity must name the manufacturer and, for imports, the EU importer. Retailers and customs ask for this file, not a certificate with a logo.

## The GPSR rule most importers miss

Since **13 December 2024**, the General Product Safety Regulation applies in the EU. It is not a toy-specific standard, but it adds requirements that break more shipments than EN71 does:

**An EU responsible person must be identified** — a natural or legal person established in the EU — on the product, the packaging, the parcel, or an accompanying document. For online sales, the information must also appear on the listing.

If you are a non-EU seller shipping to EU customers, you need this. A Chinese factory cannot be your responsible person. Neither can a freight forwarder, unless they formally accept that role.

GPSR also brings traceability requirements and mandatory reporting of accidents through the EU's Safety Business Gateway. Ask your supplier whether their packaging already carries a responsible-person block; if they export to the EU regularly, it will.

## US: the ASTM and CPC combination

The US route is a third-party tested report plus a self-issued certificate. ASTM F963-23 is the current toy standard; the CPSIA rules on lead and phthalates are the other half. Details on what gets rejected are in [our CPC guide](/blog/cpc-certificate-amazon-toys/).

The key difference from the EU: **in the US, the lab must be CPSC-accepted and the testing is mandatory for children's products.** In the EU, you can in principle self-declare without third-party testing — though no serious retailer will accept that, and it is a poor idea for anything a child mouths.

## Japan, Korea, UK in one line each

**Japan** — the ST Mark is voluntary by law but effectively required by retailers. Testing is done by an approved inspection body and takes several weeks; budget for it early if Japan is a target market.

**Korea** — the KC Mark is mandatory for toys and requires certification through a Korean safety body, with the importer often acting as the applicant. This is genuinely harder to arrange from outside Korea.

**UK** — EN71-based CE marking continues to be accepted alongside UKCA. Do not let a supplier charge you twice for a "new UK certificate" without checking whether the CE file already covers it.

## What to actually request from a factory

1. The full test report for **your SKU**, not a sample report
2. Which parts of EN71 (or F963) are covered, by number
3. The declaration of conformity, with the importer field visible
4. For the EU: confirmation of who is named as responsible person under GPSR
5. Material declarations for paint, eyes, filling and any electronic components

Point 5 is where certificates quietly stop being valid. A report covers the materials tested on the date tested. Change the paint supplier and the old report describes a product that no longer exists.

## How this affects sourcing decisions

Compliance cost is per SKU and per market, and it does not shrink with order size. A 300-piece trial order carries the same test cost as a 30,000-piece one, which is why small orders to regulated markets are disproportionately expensive — and why a supplier who already holds reports for a stock item is worth a premium.
